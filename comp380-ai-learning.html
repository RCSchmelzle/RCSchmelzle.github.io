“AI is the broadest term. Machine learning is one way we build AI. Deep learning is one approach to machine learning. So ChatGPT is AI, but AI does not mean ChatGPT.”

Tic-tac-toe — play a volunteer's moves. On every turn the AI checks each open square and applies a hand-written rule: take a win, block you, otherwise pick a neutral square at random. It only looks one move ahead, so it is beatable with a fork (two threats at once). No matter how often it loses that way, it never gets better, because it can't learn. This is AI with no learning in it at all. Worth saying out loud: for decades, this was most of AI — and classic chess engines were built the same way, just with far more moves to search and a far more elaborate hand-written scoring rule.

House prices — hit Learn. Nobody draws the line: it starts wrong and gets nudged until it fits the points. Call out the two numbers as they settle: that is what “learned” means here.

Then the learned line does something: every house more than $25k above it is overpriced, more than $25k below is underpriced, anything inside the band is on price. Drag a house up or down and watch its label flip; move a few and the line itself shifts, which relabels the others.

Deep learning — hit Train. This is real: 37 weights, trained by backpropagation right in the browser. A straight line (last example) can't split a circle; stacking layers of neurons can. Point at the small squares: each is one neuron, showing which part of the plane it responds to (blue = on, orange = the opposite). Layer 1 learns simple straight cuts; layer 2 combines them into curves. Click to add dots, or switch to corners or moons, and it re-learns. Real models have billions of weights. Go deeper
The line is blurrier than the diagram suggests. Real systems are hybrids: engineers still write the surrounding program, choose the model's architecture, pick the objective, and clean the data — all human decisions. What's learned is the parameter values. A modern LLM has billions of them; no one inspects them individually, which is why we describe these models by how they behave rather than by reading their code.

“In ordinary programming, a programmer explicitly specifies much of the procedure. In machine learning, we instead have adjustable parameters. Training finds parameter values that make the model perform well on examples.”

Emphasize: Learning = changing parameters based on data. That one sentence carries the whole first half of the deck.

How does a neural network learn?

Start with some weights. Make a prediction. See how wrong it is.

Technical detail
Backpropagation computes a gradient: for every weight, how much the loss would change if that weight were nudged slightly. It does not try out different positions. An optimizer such as gradient descent then moves each weight a small step in the direction that lowers the loss. Real training moves every weight a tiny amount at once, over many examples — not the single big jumps shown here. And the right splitter never changed because no water reached it: a weight that doesn't affect the output gets no share of the blame.
Conceptual analogy: neural networks pass numerical signals, not water. Backpropagation calculates gradients rather than physically testing different splitter positions.

“Pour. We were wrong. Look backward. Change this. Pour again. Better, still wrong. Look backward. Change this. Pour again. Correct.”

Each wedge-and-knob is one weight. Pour first and let them see the 25 / 25 / 25 / 25 miss before you say any vocabulary.

On Backpropagate, point at the orange arrows: that is error information travelling backward, not water. Say it plainly: backprop works out which knob to turn and which way; the optimizer turns it.

“You don't need the calculus today. Backprop tells us how the weights should change; optimization actually changes them.”

After TARGET REACHED, click Lock weights & use it: the same pour with every knob locked, no error and no backprop. That's inference — what happens every time you use a trained model. Then read the terms. Then the caveat: real networks pass numbers, nothing is conserved like water, and real training nudges millions of weights a little at a time.

What does each layer actually do?

It bends space. Tiny networks you can actually draw, untangling two groups until a single straight cut splits them.

Each layer does two things to every point:

  1. Stretch. Multiply by the layer's weights and add a bias. The space stretches, turns and shifts, but grid lines stay straight.
  2. Bend. Squash every value through the neuron's activation (tanh). Big values flatten toward the edges, so the grid curves.
  3. After the last layer, one straight line splits the two groups.

Each dot is an email: how many links, how many ! marks, and whether the sender is in your contacts. Spam / not spam.

The flat view uses 2 neurons per layer and both 3D views use one layer of 3, so every layer can be drawn. Real networks do the same stretching and bending in thousands of dimensions.

“So what is a layer actually doing? It's bending space. Watch.”

Before training, hit Play the bend: the random weights twist the grid, but the groups stay tangled. Then Train (about 20 seconds; the picture updates live), and play it again: layer by layer the groups get pulled apart until a single straight line splits them.

Say the two moves out loud as the slider passes them: stretch (weights and bias, straight lines stay straight) and bend (tanh squashes, lines curve). Every layer is just those two.

Then switch to 3D. The circle can't be untangled on a flat sheet, but a layer of 3 neurons lifts the dots off the page: the middle rises and the ring stays low (or the other way around), and a flat plane slides between them. Drag the picture to turn it. That's why width matters as well as depth.

Spam filter: the input axes are real features (links, ! marks, sender in contacts), and a layer of 3 neurons stretches and bends that box before one plane cuts it. Train it: the plane fades in as it gets surer. Then point at the ringed mistakes: a few real emails get filtered, a few spams get through. Those emails look identical to the others in these three features, so no amount of bending can separate them — the fix is better information (a new feature), not more layers. And who pays for each kind of mistake is a design choice.

Drag the slider to stop on any layer. The line at the end is the only “decision” the network makes; all the real work was reshaping the space first.

A tiny classifier: five labeled examples in, one new animal kept back.
Why not just test it on the examples it trained on?
Because memorizing training examples ≠ generalizing to new ones.
Overfitting: performing well on training data but poorly on new data.
Go deeper
In practice the data is split three ways: a training set that moves the parameters, a validation set for tuning choices like learning rate or when to stop, and a test set touched once, at the end. Reusing test data to make decisions quietly turns it into training data — one reason published benchmark scores drift upward. Benchmark contamination is the same problem at web scale: if the test questions appeared in the training data, the score measures recall, not generalization.

“This distinction matters whenever someone tells you an AI achieved 95 percent accuracy. Our next question should be: 95 percent on what? Training performance doesn't necessarily tell us how it handles the real world.”

Ask the “why not test on training examples?” question out loud and let them answer before you click the reveal.

Go deeper
The operation underneath almost all of it is matrix multiplication: every output number is a weighted sum, and none of them depends on the others, so thousands can be computed simultaneously. A CPU has a handful of fast, flexible cores; a GPU has thousands of simpler ones plus very high memory bandwidth to keep them fed. Training runs split work across many GPUs at once, which is why frontier training is discussed in GPU-hours and megawatts rather than lines of code.

“This is one reason modern AI is tied so closely to computing infrastructure. Training large neural networks requires staggering amounts of computation. GPUs are particularly useful because neural-network workloads involve lots of mathematical operations that can be performed in parallel.”

Say “conceptual illustration” out loud — the tick counts are a teaching device, not a measurement. Close on: algorithms + data + compute + scale.

Go deeper
Tokens come from a learned vocabulary (tens of thousands of entries) built by an algorithm like byte-pair encoding; common words are single tokens, rare ones split into pieces, and the same word can tokenize differently with or without a leading space. Each token ID indexes an embedding — a long list of numbers. Attention lets each position draw on the others, so the representation of a word depends on its context. Generation then samples from the probability distribution (temperature, top-p), which is why the same prompt can give different answers.

“An LLM doesn't operate directly on words as we see them. Text is divided into tokens, which can be words, pieces of words, punctuation, and other text units. Those are represented numerically and processed by the Transformer. Ultimately, the model produces probabilities for what token should come next.”

Then connect back to slide 4 explicitly: same prediction step — in training we measure its error and update; at inference we just take the answer.

Click a low-probability token on purpose to show that generation is a choice from a distribution.

So why might a chatbot “forget”?
The information isn't in the current context.
Older information was dropped.

Some products also have separate persistent-memory systems. Persistent memory and the active context window are different mechanisms — and products don't all implement them the same way.
Go deeper
Each request re-sends a prompt; the model holds no state between calls on its own. Attention cost grows steeply with length, so the window is finite and long chats get managed: truncation, summarization, or retrieval (fetch only the relevant pieces from a store). A persistent-memory feature is usually a database plus retrieval on top of an unchanged model — the weights stay fixed. Parameters can be changed after the fact (fine-tuning), but that is a deliberate training run, not something your chat message triggers.

“When I told the chatbot ‘Marvin,’ we generally didn't run backpropagation and retrain the neural network. Instead, chatbot software can package relevant conversation information into the model's input. The trained model then uses that information.”

Sequence: Look under the hood → Continue conversation until the window fills. Watch what happens: the Marvin turn is the oldest, so it is the first thing dropped — and the answer changes to “I don’t have that information.” Nothing was unlearned; the fact simply stopped being sent.

Then Compact context — the summary puts the fact back in far fewer tokens and the answer returns. Now ask the class why chatbots forget, and reveal the four reasons one at a time.

Do read the caveat out loud — otherwise they leave thinking every chatbot just replays the transcript.
