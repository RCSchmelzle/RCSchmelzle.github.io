What counts as AI?

How is machine learning different from normal programming?

  • Normal programming: a person writes the rules. tic-tac-toe
  • Machine learning: the rules are worked out from examples. house prices
Go deeper
In practice it's a mix. People still write the surrounding program, pick the model's shape, decide what counts as success, and gather the examples. What gets learned is the numbers inside. A modern image model has millions of them, and nobody reads them one by one, which is why we judge these models by what they do rather than by reading their code.

“What if, instead of writing the rules, we showed a computer thousands of examples and let it work the rules out?”

That's deep learning. Show it faces from more angles, in more lighting, with more skin tones, and the rules it learns cover more of the real world. The flip side: lopsided examples, lopsided rules.

Learning the rules

How does a neural network learn?

Guess. Measure the error. Adjust the knobs. Repeat.

Technical detail
Backpropagation computes a gradient: for every weight, how much the error would change if that weight were nudged slightly. It doesn't try out positions. An optimizer such as gradient descent then moves each weight a small step in the direction that lowers the error. Real training moves every weight a tiny amount at once, over many examples, not the single big jumps shown here. The right splitter never changed because no water reached it: a weight that doesn't affect the output gets no share of the blame.
An analogy. Real networks pass numbers, not water.

“Pour. Wrong. Look backward. Turn this knob. Pour again. Better, still wrong. Look backward. Turn this one. Pour again. Right.”

Four beats and nothing else: guess, measure the error, adjust the knobs, repeat. Each wedge and knob is one weight. Pour first and let them see the 25/25/25/25 miss before any vocabulary.

On Backpropagate, point at the orange arrows: error information travelling backward. Name it once: “the efficient way a network works out which knobs contributed to the error is called backpropagation. You don't need the math today.” CS majors get the term; nobody has to simulate calculus through plumbing.

After the target is reached, click Lock weights & use it: same pour, every knob locked, nothing changes. That's what happens every time a trained model is used.

Why not test it on the examples it already saw?
Because remembering the examples isn’t the same as understanding them.
Go deeper
In practice the data is split three ways: a training set that moves the parameters, a validation set for tuning choices like when to stop, and a test set touched once, at the end. Reusing test data to make decisions quietly turns it into training data, one reason published scores drift upward. If the test pictures were in the training set, the score measures memory, not understanding.

“Whenever someone says an AI is 95 percent accurate, ask: 95 percent on what? Doing well on the pictures it trained on says nothing about the real world.”

Why GPUs matter

Neural networks do enormous amounts of simple math. GPUs do lots of it at once.

CPU0 / 100 done
A few at a time. 0 conceptual ticks
A few flexible workers.
GPU0 / 100 done
Many at once. 0 conceptual ticks
Thousands of simple workers.
Neural networks
↓
Lots of matrix / numerical operations
↓
Many can happen in parallel
↓
GPUs are extremely useful
Conceptual illustration — not a benchmark
Go deeper
The operation underneath almost all of it is matrix multiplication: every output number is a weighted sum, and none of them depends on the others, so thousands can be computed at the same time. A CPU has a handful of fast, flexible cores; a GPU has thousands of simpler ones and very fast memory to keep them fed. Big training runs spread the work over many GPUs, which is why they're measured in GPU-hours and megawatts.

Optional. Skip if short on time. One sentence and the animation: that training loop is a huge pile of simple arithmetic, and GPUs can do thousands of those operations at the same time. That's why modern AI is tied to hardware, energy and who can afford it.

    Step 2 · Training

    Weights: what do they do?

    They stretch and bend the space, and training adjusts them until one flat cut splits spam from real mail.

    Each layer does two things to every point:

    1. Stretch. Multiply by the layer's weights and add a bias. The space stretches, turns and shifts, but grid lines stay straight.
    2. Bend. Squash every value through the neuron's activation (tanh). Big values flatten toward the edges, so the grid curves.
    3. After the last layer, one straight line splits the two groups.

    Each dot is an email: how suspicious its link is, how many ! marks, and whether the sender is in your contacts. Spam / not spam.

    Training data (these emails) is what the weights learn from. Test data is emails held back and never trained on, used afterward to check it works on mail it hasn't seen.

    Before training, hit Play the bend: the random starting weights twist the box, but nothing gets split and there's no plane yet. Then Train (about 20 seconds; the picture updates live) and play it again.

    Say the two moves out loud as the slider passes them: stretch (weights and bias, straight lines stay straight) and bend (tanh squashes, lines curve). Every layer is just those two.

    Spam filter: the input axes are real features (link suspicion, ! marks, sender in contacts), and a layer of 3 neurons stretches and bends that box before one plane cuts it. Train it: the plane fades in as it gets surer. Then point at the ringed mistakes: a few real emails get filtered, a few spams get through. Those emails look identical to the others in these three features, so no amount of bending can separate them — the fix is better information (a new feature), not more layers. And who pays for each kind of mistake is a design choice.

    After training, hit Unbend it (or drag the slider back): the flat wall rides back through the bend and the stretch and turns into the curved surface it really is among the original emails. A flat cut after bending is a curved cut in the space you started with.

    Drag the slider to stop on any layer. The plane at the end is the only “decision” the network makes; all the real work was reshaping the space first.
