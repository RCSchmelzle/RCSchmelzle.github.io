    Tic-tac-toe — play a volunteer's moves. On every turn the AI checks each open square and applies a hand-written rule: take a win, block you, otherwise pick a neutral square at random.     How is machine learning different from normal programming?

    • Normal programming is defined by a programmer: the rules are written ahead of time by people.
    • Machine learning is given a framework, then works out the rules on its own from examples.
    Go deeper
    The line is blurrier than the diagram suggests. Real systems are hybrids: engineers still write the surrounding program, choose the model's architecture, pick the objective, and clean the data — all human decisions. What's learned is the parameter values. A modern LLM has billions of them; no one inspects them individually, which is why we describe these models by how they behave rather than by reading their code.

    “In ordinary programming, a programmer explicitly specifies much of the procedure. In machine learning, we instead have adjustable parameters. Training finds parameter values that make the model perform well on examples.”

    One question is the whole difference: who writes the rules? Point back to the examples — the tic-tac-toe AI was all hand-written rules; the house-price line learned its own.

    Turning face rules into an algorithm

    For decades, people tried to find faces with rules like these: slide a box over the photo and check whether the dark and light areas line up like a face.

    “For a long time, this is how computers found faces. People turned 'two dark eyes, lighter nose between them' into checks a computer can run.”

    Each small tile is one check: is the black part darker than the white part? Green = yes. Stage 1 is only 9 checks, so it's cheap, and it's allowed to be wrong in the “maybe” direction.

    Honest detail: the real detector checks every position at many sizes (tens of thousands of boxes). We only show a few stops. The checks and results are the real ones (Viola–Jones, 2001).

    Seeing · the algorithm way

    But hand-made rules have limits

    Works only under some conditions
    • Face looking straight at the camera
    • Head upright, not tilted
    • Even lighting
    • Nothing covering the face
    And even then, light-and-dark rules can work unevenly across skin tones and camera exposure.
    Same real OpenCV detector, rerun on every change Photo: Sally Ride, NASA (1984), public domain

    “Those rules work, but only when the world cooperates.”

    Drag tilt: around 20 degrees it loses her. Raise the shadow from below: the eyes-darker-than-cheeks pattern breaks. Add a mask: gone. Sunglasses usually still work, since they only make the eyes darker. Worth pointing out: making the whole photo darker doesn't break it, because it compares areas to each other.

    Skin tone: these rules depend on contrast between regions, and cameras and training photos have historically been tuned for lighter skin. Face systems have measurably worked worse for some groups. The Gender Shades study (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018) found commercial face-analysis error rates up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women vs. 0.8% for lighter-skinned men.

    Bridge: writing more rules by hand for every angle, light, and face doesn't scale. So what if the rules came from examples?

    Each layer builds on the one before

    Picture: Hover a map to see which pixels it looks at.
    Low layers find small, simple things. Higher layers combine them into bigger, more meaningful ones.

    “Layer 1 finds strokes. Layer 2 looks at layer 1's maps, so it finds combinations of strokes — corners, curves, a roof peak. The last layer combines those into an answer.”

    Hover a layer-1 cell: it only sees a 3 × 3 patch. Hover a layer-2 cell: it sees 8 × 8. Each step up sees more of the picture, so it can recognize bigger things. Same idea in photo models, with dozens of layers.

    But how are these rules learned?

    Trial and error.

    How does a neural network learn?

    Start with some weights. Make a prediction. See how wrong it is.

    Technical detail
    Backpropagation computes a gradient: for every weight, how much the loss would change if that weight were nudged slightly. It does not try out different positions. An optimizer such as gradient descent then moves each weight a small step in the direction that lowers the loss. Real training moves every weight a tiny amount at once, over many examples — not the single big jumps shown here. And the right splitter never changed because no water reached it: a weight that doesn't affect the output gets no share of the blame.
    Conceptual analogy: neural networks pass numerical signals, not water. Backpropagation calculates gradients rather than physically testing different splitter positions.

    “Pour. We were wrong. Look backward. Change this. Pour again. Better, still wrong. Look backward. Change this. Pour again. Correct.”

    Start by defining parameter and landing the sentence on screen: Learning = changing parameters based on data. That one sentence carries the whole first half of the deck.

    Each wedge-and-knob is one weight. Pour first and let them see the 25 / 25 / 25 / 25 miss before you say any vocabulary.

    On Backpropagate, point at the orange arrows: that is error information travelling backward, not water. Say it plainly: backprop works out which knob to turn and which way; the optimizer turns it.

    “You don't need the calculus today. Backprop tells us how the weights should change; optimization actually changes them.”

    After TARGET REACHED, click Lock weights & use it: the same pour with every knob locked, no error and no backprop. That's inference — what happens every time you use a trained model. Then read the terms. Then the caveat: real networks pass numbers, nothing is conserved like water, and real training nudges millions of weights a little at a time.

    Training examples

    Held out

    ?
    A tiny classifier: five labeled examples in, one new animal kept back.
    Why not just test it on the examples it trained on?
    Because memorizing training examples ≠ generalizing to new ones.
    Overfitting: performing well on training data but poorly on new data.
    Go deeper
    In practice the data is split three ways: a training set that moves the parameters, a validation set for tuning choices like learning rate or when to stop, and a test set touched once, at the end. Reusing test data to make decisions quietly turns it into training data — one reason published benchmark scores drift upward. Benchmark contamination is the same problem at web scale: if the test questions appeared in the training data, the score measures recall, not generalization.

    “This distinction matters whenever someone tells you an AI achieved 95 percent accuracy. Our next question should be: 95 percent on what? Training performance doesn't necessarily tell us how it handles the real world.”

    Ask the “why not test on training examples?” question out loud and let them answer before you click the reveal.

    Why GPUs matter

    A neural network's work is an enormous pile of simple numerical operations — and most of them don't have to wait in line.

    CPU0 / 100 done
    A few at a time. 0 conceptual ticks
    Optimized for flexible, general-purpose computation.
    GPU0 / 100 done
    Many at once. 0 conceptual ticks
    Can perform huge numbers of certain numerical operations in parallel.
    Neural networks
    ↓
    Lots of matrix / numerical operations
    ↓
    Many can happen in parallel
    ↓
    GPUs are extremely useful
    Conceptual illustration — not a benchmark
    Modern AI = algorithms + data + compute + scale
    Go deeper
    The operation underneath almost all of it is matrix multiplication: every output number is a weighted sum, and none of them depends on the others, so thousands can be computed simultaneously. A CPU has a handful of fast, flexible cores; a GPU has thousands of simpler ones plus very high memory bandwidth to keep them fed. Training runs split work across many GPUs at once, which is why frontier training is discussed in GPU-hours and megawatts rather than lines of code.

    “This is one reason modern AI is tied so closely to computing infrastructure. Training large neural networks requires staggering amounts of computation. GPUs are particularly useful because neural-network workloads involve lots of mathematical operations that can be performed in parallel.”

    Say “conceptual illustration” out loud — the tick counts are a teaching device, not a measurement. Close on: algorithms + data + compute + scale.

    Step 2 · Training

    Weights: what do they do?

    They stretch and bend the space, and training adjusts them until one flat cut splits spam from real mail.

    Each layer does two things to every point:

    1. Stretch. Multiply by the layer's weights and add a bias. The space stretches, turns and shifts, but grid lines stay straight.
    2. Bend. Squash every value through the neuron's activation (tanh). Big values flatten toward the edges, so the grid curves.
    3. After the last layer, one straight line splits the two groups.

    Each dot is an email: how suspicious its link is, how many ! marks, and whether the sender is in your contacts. Spam / not spam.

    Training data (these emails) is what the weights learn from. Test data is emails held back and never trained on, used afterward to check it works on mail it hasn't seen.

    View:
    Data: drag the picture to turn it
    Made-up emails · real training
    This network has a single layer of just 3 neurons, so every step can be drawn in 3D. Real networks do the same stretching and bending in thousands of dimensions.

    “So what is a layer actually doing? It's bending space. Watch.”

    Before training, hit Play the bend: the random starting weights twist the box, but nothing gets split and there's no plane yet. Then Train (about 20 seconds; the picture updates live) and play it again.

    Say the two moves out loud as the slider passes them: stretch (weights and bias, straight lines stay straight) and bend (tanh squashes, lines curve). Every layer is just those two.

    Spam filter: the input axes are real features (link suspicion, ! marks, sender in contacts), and a layer of 3 neurons stretches and bends that box before one plane cuts it. Train it: the plane fades in as it gets surer. Then point at the ringed mistakes: a few real emails get filtered, a few spams get through. Those emails look identical to the others in these three features, so no amount of bending can separate them — the fix is better information (a new feature), not more layers. And who pays for each kind of mistake is a design choice.

    After training, hit Unbend it (or drag the slider back): the flat wall rides back through the bend and the stretch and turns into the curved surface it really is among the original emails. A flat cut after bending is a curved cut in the space you started with.

    Drag the slider to stop on any layer. The plane at the end is the only “decision” the network makes; all the real work was reshaping the space first.
